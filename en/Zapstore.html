Nostr Zapstore: The Attack on Apple & Google's App Store Monopoly

by Alien Investor

As of November 2026, Zapstore 2.0.5

Software is today's gateway to the world. But who controls which software you're allowed to install on your phone? For over a decade we've been living inside a global duopoly. Apple (iOS) and Google (Android) act as the de-facto wardens of our digital prisons — often euphemistically called "Walled Gardens."

They decide what gets censored, they take up to 30 percent as "taxes" on innovation, and they dictate what digital sovereignty is allowed to mean. But resistance is stirring. The Nostr protocol, originally launched as a Twitter alternative, has built a weapon against this monopoly: the Nostr Zapstore.

We're going to look at how this system works, why Apple is afraid of it, and whether it's actually secure.

1. The Architecture of Control: Why We Need an Alternative

To understand why the Zapstore matters, we need to name the problem. The power of Apple and Google rests on three pillars: gatekeeping, economic extraction, and data centralization.

Gatekeeping as a Power Tool

Every app must pass through corporate review. What officially goes by "security" is often political or economic censorship. A textbook example is the conflict between Apple and the Nostr app Damus.

"Apple threatened to remove Damus from the App Store because users could send each other 'Zaps' (Bitcoin via Lightning) for posts. Apple wanted such payments to go through Apple's In-App Purchase system (up to 30% cut). But since Zaps run peer-to-peer, this model doesn't technically fit cleanly. The result: Apple forced Damus to cripple the feature."

This makes it clear: real peer-to-peer economics are unwelcome in closed stores. Damus was allowed to stay, but only without zaps on posts.

The Illusion of Security

The argument "only the App Store is safe" is fragile. Centralized authorities are a single point of failure. Remember the scandal around the browser extension "Web of Trust" (WOT): a centralized tool that promised security, but was quietly passing user data to third parties in the background. When the guardian is corrupt or gets hacked, all users are affected simultaneously.

2. The Nostr Zapstore: A Technical Breakdown

The Zapstore is an app store for Android. It is built on Nostr, an open protocol, and uses several of its standards (NIPs). One catalog ships by default, the Zapstore's own. You can add others, and each catalog sets its own rules.

The Foundation: App, Release and Asset Events (NIP-82)

How do you find an app without Google? Via Nostr events following NIP-82. An app is a signed event (kind:32267) with name, icon and tags. Each release (kind:30063) points to asset events (kind:3063) with download URL and hash. These events are signed by the developer or by the catalog. Anyone can publish on Nostr, but each catalog decides what it shows. The Zapstore's catalog accepts a developer's own listings only with a current claim, so nobody can publish in the developer's name without the signing key.

Blossom: Decentralized Storage

Nostr relays typically store text, not large files. That's where Blossom comes in. Files are not stored under a name, but under their cryptographic fingerprint (hash).

  • Integrity: Change a single bit in the file and the hash changes. A modified file is caught by the check.
  • Origin: The Zapstore downloads the app file from the address in the listing, often the original source such as GitHub. If that fails, it uses the catalog's Blossom server. Where the file comes from does not matter for the check: before installing, it compares the file's hash with the signed listing.

Signature and Label

You don't need an account. Searching, installing and updating work without signing in. You only sign in to comment, share stacks or zap under your name. Your secret key stays in a signer app such as Amber, the Zapstore never sees it. Before every installation the Zapstore checks two things: the checksum of the file and the key it is signed with. If either one does not match, it installs nothing. For an update, the file must carry the same key as the installed app. This check does not yet tell you whose key that is. That is what the label is for.

  • "Claimed app" (green): the developer has proven possession of the app's signing key.
  • "Unclaimed app" (yellow): this proof is missing. In most cases the app was then not listed by the developer but by the Zapstore's catalog. It takes the app from a public source such as GitHub or F-Droid and signs the listing itself.

Our own apps are claimed in the Zapstore. A yellow label is not necessarily a bad sign. But then you have to check for yourself where the file comes from and whose key it carries. One example is Termux: the entry is unclaimed, and the file carries a test key that the Termux developers have shared publicly. With it, anyone can build an update that Android accepts. That is why you are better off getting Termux from F-Droid. There it carries F-Droid's key, and that key is not public.

3. The Money Model: Zaps Instead of Commission

Why go through the effort? Because the economic model is genuinely different.

Value4Value (V4V) Instead of a 30% Tax

The Zapstore takes no cut. A zap is a tip via Bitcoin Lightning. For claimed apps it goes directly to the developer, otherwise to the catalog that listed the app. The app page tells you whom you are supporting. The Zapstore does not handle the payment itself.

Web of Trust Instead of Algorithm

The Zapstore does not rate apps. For claimed apps, the install sheet shows who follows the developer on Nostr, optionally from the point of view of the people you follow. According to the Zapstore, a follow is not an endorsement. On top of that there are comments and stacks, lists of apps that others have put together. The social features can be switched off.

4. Decentralization vs. Security: A Critical Look

Nobody reviews apps in the Zapstore by hand. So how does it protect us from malware?

Feature Apple / Google (Centralized) Nostr Zapstore (Decentralized)
Trust You trust the brand (blind trust). You check label, source and signing key.
Censorship Global kill-switch possible. Each catalog sets its own rules. You can add others or run your own.
Malware protection Automated scanners & review teams. No manual review. Checksum and key before every installation, the catalog's security report, Android sandbox.

Protection in the Zapstore is based on checks and transparency. If the developer's proof is missing, the app shows the yellow "Unclaimed app" label. Also: on Android, apps run in a sandbox. Even a malicious app from the Zapstore cannot access your contacts or location data without the corresponding permissions. The operating system's security is the last line of defense.

5. In Practice: Does This Actually Work?

Android: The Happy Path

On Android, the Zapstore already works surprisingly well today. It needs Android 10 or later and a phone with a 64-bit ARM processor. Once, you allow it to install apps. After that it feels like an F-Droid store with social features. In the settings you choose how often it checks for updates in the background (every hour by default) and whether it may use mobile data for that. Whenever Android allows it, it installs updates without asking. With the zsp tool, developers publish their apps themselves, automatically from their CI pipeline if they like (e.g. GitHub Actions). Nobody reviews this by hand. The catalog reads every file itself and checks the signing key, the architecture and the version code.

iOS: Sovereignty Denied

The Zapstore is available for Android only. With version 1.1 the project dropped Linux and macOS as platforms, and zapstore.dev offers no iOS version. Apple allows third-party app stores only in a few regions, such as the EU, Japan and Brazil. Even there, Apple has to authorize each store first. This makes it clear: anyone who wants digital freedom hits a hard wall with Apple.

6. Alien Verdict: A Crack in the Wall

The Nostr Zapstore isn't ready for mass adoption yet. It doesn't pass the "grandma test." But it's proof that it's technically possible to distribute software globally without asking a corporation for permission and without paying up to 30 percent protection money.

It's a return to the principles of the open internet — combined with hard money (Bitcoin) and hard cryptography. For developers it's a lifeboat. For users it's a tool for self-defense.
