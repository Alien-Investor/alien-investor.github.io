Alien Analyzer V2 – Stock Analysis in 4 Tabs Explained

by Alien Investor · Updated: 4 November 2026

No Bloomberg terminal, no subscription, no login. The Alien Analyzer V2 is a free value-investing tool that runs right in your browser. Enter a ticker and within seconds you see how a stock is valued against its own history, what it pays out and how solid the numbers behind it are.

Prices and metrics come from Yahoo Finance. For companies that file annual reports with the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), the analyzer also uses the figures from those reports, so the history often reaches back ten years instead of about four. My own server fetches both: your browser does not contact any third-party data service, and fonts and the chart library come from the same server.

Quick start

  1. Find a stock: type a ticker or company name, for example SAP, ALV.DE or NESN.SW. Suggestions appear as you type. Press Enter or “Analyse” to start.
  2. Read the header: price with the change from the previous month (1M), market cap, P/E and earnings per share over the last twelve months.
  3. Click through the tabs: Fair Value, Multiples, Dividends, Quality Check. The small ? in each tab jumps straight to the matching section of this guide. If something about a stock's data needs explaining, Kerstin's box appears below it.
  1. Search field: ticker or name, suggestions appear as you type.
  2. Header: price and change from the previous month, market cap, P/E (trailing) and earnings per share over the last twelve months (EPS ttm).
  3. Language and theme: DE/EN switches the language, DARK/SOFT switches between a black and a dark blue background.

Tab 1 – Fair Value

The most important tab for value investors. It answers one question: where would the price be if the market valued the stock today the way it did on average over the last ten years?

Fair value = earnings per share (last twelve months) × median P/E (last ten years)
  1. Time range: 5, 8, 10, 15 or 20 years or the full history. The buttons only change the visible section. The fair value is always calculated over the last ten years, so its result does not depend on the button you picked.
  2. Price: the white line, one value per month.
  3. Fair value line: dashed in orange, earnings per share of each month times the median P/E. Tap the line to see the median and the month from which it applies.
  4. P/E fan: earnings per share times 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30, from green to red. It shows at which P/E the price currently sits.
  5. Sidebar: P/E, P/B and P/S, each as “Avg.” (the median) and “Cur.” (the current value). “P/E avg. since 12/2016” names the first month that goes into the median.
  6. Fair value indicator: the result in one line.

Where earnings per share come from. For each fiscal year the analyzer takes diluted earnings per share from the annual report and connects the years in a straight line. After the last report the line runs to the earnings of the last twelve months. For every month it divides the price by these earnings; the median of these monthly values is the “Ø”. Yahoo Finance provides about four fiscal years. For companies with annual reports at the SEC (US stocks, ADRs and some home listings such as SAP.DE) the analyzer puts the older years from those reports in front, and the series then usually covers the full ten years.

What the fair value indicator can show

  • ▲ …% above fair value or ▼ …% below fair value: the price is more than 15% above or below the fair value.
  • ≈ fairly valued: price and fair value are no more than 15% apart.
  • No P/E model: for REITs (FFO is what counts there) and commodity miners (P/B and production costs there), because P/E says little about them.
  • Distorted: more than a quarter of pre-tax earnings does not come from operations, for example from selling a stake. “Fairly valued” or “below fair value” would then look too cheap. “Above fair value” stays.
  • No fair value: for example after a loss over the last twelve months, with fewer than three consecutive annual profits, or when the median P/E is above 60. The reason is shown alongside.

Good to know

Loss years: the analyzer bridges a single loss year or missing year. With two or more in between there is no P/E value for that stretch, and a fair value only appears once there are three consecutive annual profits again. It does not extrapolate earnings back before the first annual profit.

Tab 2 – Multiples

A single snapshot says little. What matters is how it compares with the company's own history. The tab shows four valuation metrics as lines: P/E (price ÷ earnings per share), P/B (price ÷ book value per share), P/S (price ÷ sales per share) and P/CF (price ÷ operating cash flow per share).

  1. Time range: 5 or 10 years or the full history. Here the button also changes the Ø.
  2. Legend: current value and, in brackets, the Ø (median) for the selected range.
  3. Line: the monthly value of the metric, here the P/E.
  4. Dashed line: the Ø in the same colour.
  5. Later start: Exxon's P/S only starts in 2023. For earlier years the revenue figures in the SEC reports did not match those from Yahoo Finance, which is common for oil and gas companies. The analyzer then calculates nothing there.

How the analyzer calculates. It works out earnings, book value, sales and operating cash flow per share for each fiscal year with that year's share count, not today's. Otherwise companies that buy back shares would look cheaper in the past than they were. After the last annual report come the latest quarterly balance sheets and sales and cash flow over the last twelve months.

“Ø” is always the median of the monthly values, not the mean: a few outliers hardly move it. For P/E only monthly values above 3 count, and upward outliers (above 2.5 times the median, at least 50) are left out. If the median of P/B, P/S or P/CF is above 100, the analyzer shows no Ø.

Good to know

If a large share of P/E values is filtered out, for example after big write-downs, Kerstin shows a warning in the tab. The stock may then fit a plain P/E grid poorly. For banks the analyzer shows no P/CF average: their operating cash flow moves mainly with trading assets.

Tab 3 – Dividends

The key tab for dividend investors: what does the company pay, how high is the yield compared with the past, and is the dividend growing?

  1. Time range: 5, 10 or 20 years.
  2. Bars: dividend per share in the month of the ex-date (right axis).
  3. Yield: dividends over the last twelve months divided by the month's price (left axis).
  4. Corridor: the blue band shows how much the yield swung around its mean over the preceding year (mean ± one standard deviation).
  5. Current yield: payments over the last twelve months divided by the current price.
  6. Dividend growth: how this twelve-month sum has changed, compared with twelve months ago and as an annual rate over 2, 5 and 10 years.

Good to know

Twelve months instead of “last annual dividend”: the analyzer counts every payment whose ex-date falls within the last twelve months, with some leeway for shifted dates. If the same payment date ends up in the window twice as a result, only the more recent one counts. Without a payment in twelve months the line shows no yield, for example when a company has suspended its dividend.

Gap and double-height bar: at Realty Income one ex-date fell on 1 November 2021 instead of the end of October. October therefore stays empty and November's bar holds two payments.

ADRs and dual listings: for some US listings of foreign companies whose dividend history at Yahoo Finance has gaps or deviations, the analyzer converts the payments on the home exchange using the exchange rate on the ex-date and, for ADRs, the number of shares per ADR (a dual listing such as Enbridge is the same share). Kerstin then names the home listing in the tab and, where one ADR represents several shares, the ratio.

Tab 4 – Quality Check

Numbers alone are not enough. The Quality Check sorts ten metrics into a traffic-light table, each with a clear signal.

  1. Benchmark: what the value is measured against; for P/E the company's own Ø and the sector P/E (“S”).
  2. Signal: the traffic light by fixed thresholds per metric. Green is the better end of the scale, red the worse, yellow is in between or marks a special case. “N/A” means the metric does not fit here or data is missing.
  3. Context: a short sentence on the signal.
  4. Balance sheet date: the date of the balance sheet behind the equity ratio and P/B. It can be older than the earnings figures.
  5. Alien Assessment: the text on the stock, see the next section.
  6. Note: “Automatic assessment by fixed rules, not a recommendation to buy or sell”, with a link to all the rules.

Good to know

If a one-off effect inflates earnings, P/E and a high return on equity show “DISTORTED”. A “HIGH” P/E stays. For banks the Quality Check does not rate free cash flow (“N/A (BANK)”), and the same goes for BDCs, which book the purchase of their investments in operating cash flow.

Kerstin's notes

Kerstin, the onboard AI from my books, only speaks up in a tab when something about the selected stock's data or its industry is special. Her box sits below the time range buttons and is collapsed; tapping the button opens it. A warning sign on the button means at least one note affects how meaningful a ratio is. Kerstin describes the data, she doesn't rate the stock. For most stocks the box doesn't appear.

  • Fair Value: banks (Yahoo's revenue is essentially a net figure, so their P/S can't be compared with other industries), a gap of more than one fiscal year in the earnings series (losses or no report) and financial statements in another currency without a matching exchange rate, as well as oil and gas companies, fertilizer producers and shipping companies, whose earnings swing with commodity prices or freight rates, and with them the fair value.
  • Multiples: many P/E values outside the plotted range, and a share count that has more than doubled or halved since the last fiscal year. The analyzer then shows no average for P/B, P/S and P/CF. For REITs, Kerstin explains why depreciation or revaluation of the properties moves the P/E line without any cash moving, and that the industry uses adjusted measures such as FFO.
  • Dividends: banks and insurers, because a special dividend can lift the yield over the last twelve months, and dividends the analyzer converts from the home exchange.

At the bottom of the box, “More in the guide” leads to the section for that tab.

Alien Assessment

Below the traffic-light table is the Alien Assessment: an automatically generated text that describes where the stock stands against its history and what needs to be read differently for this company. The analyzer detects special cases in a fixed order, specific before general, for example:

  • REITs: P/E is distorted by depreciation, FFO is the yardstick.
  • Banks: a low equity ratio is no warning sign; bank-specific metrics such as net interest margin and CET1 ratio matter.
  • BDCs: net asset value (NAV) and how well net investment income covers the dividend.
  • Commodity producers and fertilizer makers: cyclical earnings distort the multiples.
  • Royalty companies, pipelines, luxury brands: business models with their own valuation logic.
  • Bitcoin vehicles such as Strategy (MSTR): not a conventional company, the yardstick is the premium or discount to its bitcoin holdings. The analyzer does not calculate it, because it does not read in the number of bitcoins held. It shows P/B instead: book value consists mainly of bitcoin and cash, less debt and preferred shares.

The assessment describes, it does not recommend. It knows neither you nor your goals. All rules and thresholds are on the methodology page: How the analyzer calculates.

FAQ

Which stocks can I analyse?

Anything with a ticker on Yahoo Finance: US stocks directly (AAPL), other exchanges with a suffix, for example .DE for Xetra, .SW for Zurich, .L for London, .PA for Paris, .AS for Amsterdam or .T for Tokyo. For some secondary listings with patchy data, a dialog points you to the main listing.

Why does the average P/E differ from other websites?

Other sites often use the mean instead of the median, a different period, basic instead of diluted earnings, or earnings excluding one-off items. Without SEC reports the analyzer's series also only reaches back about four years, so the average often covers only part of a cycle.

What does “P/E avg. since 01/2023” mean?

Real earnings figures exist from that month on; the analyzer does not extrapolate earnings back before it. If the month is well after ten years ago, the Ø rests on correspondingly fewer years, usually because there are no SEC reports (for example Nestlé or LVMH).

Why does the fair value not change when I switch the time range?

That is intentional. The fair value is always calculated over the last ten years; the buttons only change the visible section. That way the result does not depend on which button you pressed last. In the Multiples tab, by contrast, the Ø follows the selected range.

Why is there no fair value?

For REITs and commodity miners P/E says little, so there is no P/E model. Otherwise the fair value is missing, for example, after a loss over the last twelve months, with fewer than three consecutive annual profits, or when the median P/E is above 60. The fair value indicator always names the reason.

Who sees which stocks I look at?

There is no account and no login. Your browser loads the page, fonts and data only from my server. My server therefore sees which tickers are requested; Yahoo Finance and the SEC only receive its requests, not yours. Your browser stores language and theme locally.

Is this investment advice?

No. The analyzer calculates by fixed rules and describes where metrics stand against their history. It does not know your situation, and data can be wrong. It is a scan tool for a first look, not a replacement for your own research.

I found an error. What should I do?

At the bottom of the analyzer there is “Report a bug”. It opens a short form with the ticker filled in; “Send” turns it into a prepared email to me. One sentence about what you noticed is enough.

The Alien Analyzer V2 is not a replacement for your own research and not a trading signal. It is a scan tool: fast, free, no registration. Enter a ticker, click through four tabs, and you know where a stock stands against its own history before you dig deeper.
