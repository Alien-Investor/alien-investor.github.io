Commodity Super-Cycle 2026 – Selective, Structural, Substantial

By Alien Investor – As of: early 2026, revised on 1 Nov 2026 (oil and gas, uranium, production costs, new section “My Commodity Basket”). Figures without their own date are from spring 2026.

Everyone is talking about the commodity super-cycle. Some are celebrating the next 2000–2011 boom, where shovels, barrels, and grains all explode at once. Others wave it off: China hype, already priced in. Both are wrong. The truth is more selective – and precisely because of that, more interesting for anyone who wants to understand what is structurally happening.

This analysis looks at the macro drivers, the individual commodity classes, and concrete investment vehicles – through the lens of a long-term co-owner, not a trader chasing the next monthly chart.

"There is no new China. But there are copper cables without which no nuclear reactor runs, and gold that no central bank willingly gives up. That's enough for a thesis."

The Alien Verdict in One Sentence

The super-cycle is not a monolithic phenomenon – it is selective and concentrated in copper, gold, silver, and uranium: four commodities where decades of underinvestment, structurally rising demand, and near-impossible substitution all converge simultaneously.

1) The Macro Framework: Tailwinds from Three Directions

Commodity super-cycles do not emerge in a vacuum. They need macroeconomic fuel. And right now that fuel exists in an unusually concentrated form.

USD weakness and fiscal dominance: Historically, commodity super-cycles correlate strongly with phases of structural US dollar weakness. The DXY fell roughly 10.7 percent in the first half of 2025 – the sharpest decline over such a period in more than 50 years. At the start of 2026 it trades at around 97.65. The drivers are not short-term interest rate differentials but fundamental fiscal concerns: the US deficit stands at nearly 6 percent of GDP, net interest costs have crossed the one-trillion-dollar mark for the first time, and Moody's downgraded the US credit rating to Aa1 in May 2025. Economists call this pattern "fiscal dominance" – when political pressure for low rates permanently dominates monetary policy and thereby cements structurally weaker purchasing power of the dollar. For commodities priced in dollars, a weaker dollar is a lasting price tailwind.

Geopolitical fragmentation as a supply tightener: What once optimized global supply chains is fragmenting fast. More than 100 trade restrictions on critical minerals are in force globally. Indonesia's nickel ore export ban, Chile's state control of lithium, DRC cobalt export blocks, Tanzania's beneficiation requirements – resource nationalism is no longer an exception, it is strategy. Add reshoring and friendshoring: bringing factories back home requires commodities – and that is happening amid slowing global growth.

The ESG paradox: The green transition is the largest commodity program in history. The IEA expects demand for critical minerals to triple by 2030. At the same time, environmental permitting in Western countries delays mine development by 10 to 15 years of lead time. Global exploration budgets for nonferrous metals including gold and silver stood at around $12.4 billion in 2025 according to S&P Global. For 2026 companies have budgeted around a third more, $16.5 billion, still below the 2012 high of $20.5 billion. The plan is to cap supply and triple demand. That does not add up.

2) The Super-Cycle is Selective – A Framework

The key difference from the super-cycle of the 2000s: back then, China was the demand driver that needed everything at once – steel, copper, oil, wheat, soybeans. A new China with comparable demand breadth and purchasing power does not exist. Instead there is a differentiated cycle driven by three forces: the electrification of the economy, the nuclear renaissance, and the monetary reordering through de-dollarization.

The rating matrix is clear: a genuine super-cycle requires three conditions simultaneously – decades of investment deficits with extremely long lead times for new supply, structurally rising demand, and limited substitution options. Copper, gold, silver, and uranium meet all three criteria. Oil, gas, and most agricultural commodities do not. For oil and gas a different argument carries, the vulnerability of supply (section 8).

3) Copper – the Flagship of the Super-Cycle

Copper is the red thread running through the entire energy transition. No wind turbine, no EV, no solar panel, no data center without copper. Global demand in 2024 stands at around 27 million tonnes. BHP projects over 50 million tonnes per year by 2050. Data centers alone will absorb an additional 250,000 to 550,000 tonnes by 2030, according to the IEA.

On the supply side the picture is brutal: mine ore grades have fallen roughly 40 percent since 1991. Development timelines for new mines run 16 to 25 years. The IEA expects mine production to peak in the late 2020s at around 24 million tonnes. BloombergNEF puts the cumulative deficit through 2050 at 19 million tonnes – without massive expansion of mines and recycling. Aluminum cannot meaningfully substitute copper in high-performance applications. Recycling supplies around 20 percent of the market – growing, but not enough.

4) Gold – the Monetary Reordering in Action

Gold is no longer just a "safe haven" – it is undergoing a qualitative shift in its demand structure. Central banks bought more than 1,000 tonnes net for three consecutive years from 2022 to 2024 – twice the rate of the previous decade. 76 percent of central banks plan further reserve increases over the next five years, according to the World Gold Council. The USD share of global foreign exchange reserves has fallen to around 57 percent. That is de-dollarization in slow motion.

Add to that peak gold: since 2020 there have been only five significant new discoveries – in the 1990s there were still 132. Average ore grades declined from 1.31 g/t (2012) to 1.14 g/t (2022). All-in sustaining costs (AISC) averaged around $1,785 per ounce in the first quarter of 2026 according to the World Gold Council, a good third of the average gold price in that quarter. The gold price reached a new all-time high in January 2026 above $5,595 per ounce.

The classic counter-argument – Bitcoin as digital gold – did not hold in 2025. Bitcoin lost more than 30 percent from its all-time high while gold gained around 55 percent. No central bank holds Bitcoin in its reserves, the Czech National Bank has only been testing a small portfolio of digital assets including Bitcoin outside its reserves since November 2025.

5) Silver – the Underrated Double Act

Silver is simultaneously a monetary safe haven and an industrial high-performance commodity – with the highest electrical conductivity of any element. This dual role is its strength, but it also makes it more volatile than gold.

The structural argument is compelling: more than 70 percent of silver mine production comes as a by-product of copper, lead, and zinc mining. Higher silver prices therefore structurally trigger very little additional production – a classic supply cap. At the same time, industrial demand hit an all-time high in 2024 at 680 million ounces. Solar PV alone absorbs around 197 million ounces – 29 percent of industrial demand, versus 11 percent a decade ago. The cumulative deficit from 2021 to 2025 amounts to roughly 820 million ounces – a full year of mine production. The price broke through the 1980 high in 2025 and is trading above $80 per ounce at the start of 2026.

6) Uranium – the Most Textbook Super-Cycle Setup

If you are looking for a textbook example of a super-cycle setup, uranium is the candidate. Primary production in 2024 covered only around 90 percent of reactor demand. The rest comes from dwindling secondary sources – a buffer that is running down.

On the demand side an unusually large number of forces are converging at once: COP28 commitments to triple nuclear capacity, EU taxonomy inclusion, the US ADVANCE Act, 65 reactors under construction, 95 in planning. And the decisive new element: the power appetite of the AI industry. Microsoft, Google (Alphabet) and Amazon are signing long-term off-take agreements with existing nuclear plants – because no other carbon-free baseload system delivers the required reliability.

Kazatomprom, the world's largest uranium producer with around 21 percent of world output, mines in Kazakhstan, which supplied around 39 percent of the world's uranium in 2024 according to the World Nuclear Association. For 2026 the company plans around 10 percent less output than its previous licensed levels, because in its view the market balance does not justify a return to full output. New mines require 10 to 15 years of lead time.

7) Lithium – Growth Yes, Super-Cycle Doubtful

Lithium is the complicated story in this picture. The structural demand is real: around 15.7 percent CAGR through 2030 driven by EV mandates and stationary storage. The IEA sees a deficit of just under 100,000 tonnes LCE equivalent by 2030. But lithium has a problem that copper does not: supply is more flexible. Brine production, new DLE technology, and faster project scaling enable a more dynamic supply response.

That was demonstrated in 2024/25: spot prices plunged from above $78,000 per tonne (end of 2022) to around $8,000 (mid-2025), before recovering to roughly $13,000. The pattern points to recurring boom-bust cycles – not a steady structural uptrend. Strategic positions remain interesting: the US government holds a 5 percent stake in Lithium Americas, Rio Tinto acquired Arcadium Lithium for $6.7 billion. That shows the strategic importance – but caution is warranted.

8) Where the Super-Cycle Does Not Hold: Nickel, Oil, Agriculture (broad)

Nickel faces structural oversupply. Indonesia controls 51 percent of global mine production, of which roughly 70 percent is under Chinese control. Macquarie projects a surplus through at least 2030 – potentially nine consecutive years of oversupply. Add LFP substitution: nickel-free battery chemistries already dominate in China. The LME price sits roughly 70 percent below the 2022 high. BHP suspended Nickel West. Not a pretty picture.

Oil has no permanent deficit. Before the war in the Gulf the market was in surplus, and for 2027 the IEA and the US Energy Information Administration again expect a surplus. The IEA's medium-term report (“Oil 2025”) saw demand settling at around 105 million barrels per day through 2030, and over 17 million electric vehicles were sold in 2024. Whether demand then falls or keeps growing until 2050 depends above all on policy in the scenarios of the World Energy Outlook 2025.

What carries for oil and gas is the vulnerability of supply. Without ongoing investment, global oil production would fall by around eight percent a year according to the IEA, and almost nine out of ten dollars invested in oil and gas production since 2019 merely offset the decline of existing fields. At the same time this investment lies around a third below its 2015 level. In normal times about a fifth of global oil consumption and of LNG trade passes through the Strait of Hormuz, and most of the spare production capacity also lies in the Gulf. Since the attack by the US and Israel on Iran on 28 February 2026 the strait has been heavily restricted, and the IEA speaks of the largest supply disruption in the history of the oil market. At the end of September 2026 global oil stocks were around 509 million barrels below their pre-war level according to the IEA.

That is not a super-cycle, rather a cycle with built-in shock risk. I still hold oil and gas, as a small bet on underinvestment and supply shocks via a broad energy ETF in my commodity basket (section 10). An oil company is not an oil price, it remains a business.

For agricultural commodities, technology (precision farming, CRISPR genome editing, improved seed varieties) largely offsets demand growth. Selective bottlenecks exist in fertilizers (geopolitical concentration in potash and phosphate) and tropical soft commodities such as cocoa. For broadly diversified agriculture ETFs, the super-cycle foundation is absent.

9) How to Position – a Mental Framework

Not every commodity investment suits every owner type equally. A conceptual structure, meant for the commodity part of a portfolio, not for its core:

  • Conservative (calmest part of a commodity allocation): Royalty and streaming companies such as Franco-Nevada, Wheaton Precious Metals, or Royal Gold offer broad commodity exposure with higher balance sheet quality, lower operational risk, and no direct mine execution risk.
  • Balanced (quality producers): Agnico Eagle (gold), Cameco (uranium), Freeport-McMoRan or BHP (copper) – established producers with stable cash flows that benefit directly from rising commodity prices.
  • Leveraged (developers and juniors): NexGen Energy (uranium), Hudbay Minerals (copper), or physical funds such as Sprott Physical Uranium Trust – higher volatility, significantly higher upside when price moves are confirmed. Only with a correspondingly long horizon and risk tolerance.
  • Broadly diversified: ETFs covering the entire commodity sector or specific sub-classes (e.g. Global X Uranium UCITS ETF, Global X Copper Miners UCITS ETF, my six funds are listed in the next section) as a convenient option without individual stock selection. Broad does not mean calm, the volatility of the sector remains.

10) My Commodity Basket: Core and Satellite

Besides gold and silver in my own safe, I also hold commodities on the stock exchange, as equity ETFs on producing companies. Behind this is a core-and-satellite setup. The core is a broad index fund, in my case the Vanguard FTSE All-World. Alongside it sit directly registered individual shares and smaller building blocks for a deliberate conviction of my own. For me that is commodities, because without metals and energy neither power grids nor data centers nor factories run, and because supply is fragile after years of underinvestment. The analog reserve remains physical gold and silver, the stock-market part does not replace it.

This is a sector bet, and I call it that. I remain critical of thematic ETFs on hydrogen, climate or artificial intelligence. Research does not spare sector funds either, though: a study in the Review of Financial Studies analyzed US equity ETFs from 2000 to 2019. Specialized funds lost around 30 percent on a risk-adjusted basis in their first five years, and sector funds did barely better than thematic funds. What mattered was timing, because new funds arrived when their theme was already expensive. That is why I build up the positions through savings plans, spread over the cycle, instead of buying them in one go during a boom.

Three worked examples of a split

How you weight the building blocks and what amount you invest is a personal decision. Three worked examples, not a recommendation:

  • Equal weight: each of the six building blocks one-sixth. That is simple and needs no view on individual commodities. The catch: gold is in it twice, via the two gold miners funds.
  • Precious-metals focus: large gold miners 25 percent, junior miners 15, silver 20, copper 15, uranium 10, energy 15. Gold and silver, at 60 percent, form the heavyweight of the basket, with industrial metals and energy mixed in.
  • Less overlap: large gold miners 30 percent, silver 20, copper 20, uranium 15, energy 15. The junior fund drops out, because a good three-quarters of its weight sits in firms that the large gold miners fund also holds. Fewer building blocks also mean less administration.

Volatility is no yardstick here. The mining and uranium funds all swing hard, the energy fund with the large oil companies considerably less. Annualized volatility over three years according to justETF, as of late October 2026: silver miners around 41 percent, uranium 38, junior gold miners 36, large gold miners 34, copper 30, energy 19. None of the worked examples is weighted by volatility. They follow simplicity, your own conviction or the overlap.

Leverage and tax

What speaks for the basket is at the same time its risk: leverage. A mine earns on the difference between the metal price and production costs, and this difference swings more sharply than the price itself. If sustaining costs lie at a good third of the gold price, as in the first quarter of 2026, the margin grows by around 16 percent when the gold price rises ten percent. The same leverage works downward: from 2011 to early 2016 gold lost around 44 percent, the index of the large gold miners around 80 percent. A broad gold miners ETF went through that crash as well.

An ETF is also not ownership of the mines but a unit in a fund, and the voting rights are exercised by the fund company. For German tax purposes a commodity ETF is not gold: gains are capital income and, unlike with physical gold, do not become tax-free even after more than a year. The partial exemption softens this, and because all six funds accumulate, the advance lump sum can fall due every year. The bigger picture is in my book “The Decentralized System”, Chapters 13, 17 and 18.

11) Alien Verdict

The commodity super-cycle is not a simple "buy everything that comes out of the ground." It is selective, structurally grounded, and runs on a macro foundation of dollar weakness, fiscal dominance, and geopolitical fragmentation – all forces that do not resolve in a single quarter.

Copper is the backbone of electrification and has the strongest deficit argument. Gold is the monetary reordering in action – not speculation, but central bank strategy in real time. Silver is copper and gold combined with higher leverage. Uranium is the most textbook super-cycle thesis with the clearest supply-demand picture.

Nickel and broad agriculture lack comparable structural arguments. For oil and gas it is not scarcity that carries but the vulnerability of supply. There, the bet is on a cycle with shock risk, not on a super-cycle. That is a decisive difference for the long-term owner. I still hold oil and gas as a small building block in my commodity basket, deliberately as a bet. For me the whole stock-market part is a satellite next to the broad index fund as the core, and the analog reserve remains physical gold and silver.

"Invest in the scarcities of the future, not the surpluses of the past. And always verify for yourself whether the thesis still holds."
