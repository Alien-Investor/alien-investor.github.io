How my analyses are made

by Alien Investor · as of 30 Oct 2026

This page explains how my stock analyses, my Bitcoin analyses and the Alien Analyzer work: where the numbers come from, what is fact and what is my assessment, which assumptions sit behind a fair value, and where I have interests of my own. Every analysis links to this page.

What an analysis is and what it is not

  • An analysis is my personal assessment of a company or of Bitcoin, based on publicly available data.
  • It is not investment advice. I do not know your financial situation, your goals or your risk tolerance, and I do not take them into account. No analysis says that a stock or Bitcoin suits you.
  • Analyses are not updated continuously. The date stated in the analysis applies. Prices, metrics and my assessment may change afterwards.
  • Stocks and Bitcoin can fluctuate sharply. Losses up to a total loss are possible.

Facts, estimates, opinion

Every analysis contains three kinds of statements. I keep them apart:

KindExamplesHow it is marked
FactsRevenue, earnings, debt, dividends, segment figuresfrom annual reports and mandatory filings, with the data date
EstimatesFair value, price scenarios, forecasts, analyst expectationslabelled as a model calculation or estimate, with the assumptions; third-party forecasts with source
OpinionMoat, management, risks, the Alien verdictworded as my assessment

Values I cannot back up with a source are marked as an estimate or as not available, instead of inventing a number.

Sources

  • Primary sources: annual reports, annual and quarterly filings and other mandatory disclosures (for US companies via SEC EDGAR), the companies' investor relations pages, official statistics.
  • Metrics and price history: my own Alien Analyzer V2, which processes data from Yahoo Finance. This data can contain errors, for example from one-off accounting effects or financial data in a different currency than the share price. I check conspicuous values against the annual reports and say so in the text when doubts remain.
  • Research: research tools, including AI-assisted ones, help with collecting and sorting. The primary sources are what counts: numbers and factual statements are checked against them before publication.
  • Blog posts and forums are not a source for numbers.

How I value

Fair value

The fair value in my analyses is a model calculation, not a price target. It is based on the fair value indicator of the Alien Analyzer:

Fair value = current earnings per share × median P/E ratio over the months with actual earnings figures (about four years at Yahoo Finance)

The assumptions behind it:

  • The market will value the company in the future roughly as it did on average in the past.
  • Current earnings are representative of the company's earning power.

Both can be wrong: for cyclicals at an earnings peak or trough, after one-off effects, when growth breaks or when the interest rate environment changes. That is why I check the indicator in every analysis against the P/E history of recent years and put it into context in the text instead of taking it over unchecked. When an analysis names a price scenario, its assumptions are stated in the text.

Metrics and quality check

For context I use historical valuation multiples (P/E, P/CF, P/S, P/B), the dividend history and a quality check covering return on equity, debt, free cash flow, equity ratio, earnings growth, dividend yield and the change in share count. The traffic-light colours in the analyzer follow fixed rules: mostly fixed thresholds, for P/E a comparison with the stock's own average and the sector P/E, and special rules for some sectors. For banks, insurers, REITs, commodity companies and other special cases, some metrics do not fit. The analysis says so. For banks and BDCs, the quality check does not rate free cash flow ("N/A"): at banks, operating cash flow swings mainly with trading assets, and BDCs book the purchase of their investments there. The investment banks Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley also count as banks.

Bitcoin analyses

Bitcoin is not a stock. There are no earnings and no P/E ratio. My Bitcoin analyses are based on the price history, on the power law model and on publicly available market data.

  • Trend: price in USD = 1.0117 × 10-17 × (days since 3 January 2009)5.82 (power law model after Giovanni Santostasi, in a widely used calibration; newer estimates give a slightly lower exponent).
  • Support: 0.42 × trend (floor from the same calibration). Between support and trend lies the corridor in which the price has often moved so far.
  • The model describes the past. No mechanism guarantees that it will keep holding. Price scenarios from the model are estimates.

I hold Bitcoin. Every Bitcoin analysis says so as well.

Conflicts of interest

  • Every analysis has a dated disclosure block: whether I or persons closely associated with me hold shares of the company, and whether I have a business relationship with it.
  • I receive no money from the companies I analyse. The only exception is Amazon: I receive royalties for my books through Kindle Direct Publishing. The Amazon analysis says so.
  • My income: books (Amazon and direct sales), affiliate links (marked with *), donations and services. The YouTube channel is not monetised.
  • Requested analyses: A reader pays for an analysis of a stock of his choice. He only chooses the company. He has no influence on content or result, and his personal situation plays no role. A paid analysis is marked as such in the disclosure.

Date and timeliness

The top of every analysis states the data date. Prices and metrics refer to that point in time. The disclosure block states the date and time of publication, after a full revision also the time of that revision, plus the date of the disclosure itself.

Alien Analyzer

The Alien Analyzer V2 calculates automatically for every ticker you enter. It knows neither you nor your goals.

  • Data: Yahoo Finance, fetched through my own server. For companies that file annual reports with the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), i.e. US stocks, ADRs and some home listings of such companies such as SAP.DE, ASML.AS or 6758.T, the server also uses the XBRL data from these reports (see P/E median). Your browser does not query any third-party data services. The quality check states the date of the balance sheet behind the equity ratio and P/B. It can be older than the earnings data. If a company reports in a different currency than its share price, such as Chinese ADRs in renminbi or Novo Nordisk in Danish kroner, the analyzer converts earnings per share, book value, revenue and cash flow into the price currency using exchange rates from Yahoo Finance, the history at the rate of each month. The free cash flow row names the original currency. If Yahoo Finance delivers part of the financial figures in a different currency than the rest, the analyzer first converts these values into the currency of the other figures at the exchange rate of the respective reporting date. For Petrobras this affects balance sheet and cash flow, for Vale also earnings: they come in US dollars, the rest in reais.
  • Fair value indicator: formula as above. Current earnings per share are the earnings of the last twelve months according to Yahoo Finance. After the last annual report, the analyzer does not extrapolate with a growth rate but connects it to this twelve-month figure. If the price is more than 15% above or below fair value, the analyzer shows "above" or "below fair value", otherwise "fairly valued". For comparison, the P/E fan shows earnings per share times 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30. For REITs and mining and metals companies the analyzer shows no fair value, because the P/E ratio is of little use there, nor when the median P/E is above 60, when Yahoo Finance provides fewer than three positive annual earnings figures or when the company made a loss over the last twelve months.
  • P/E median: For each month the analyzer divides the price by earnings per share. Yahoo Finance provides about four years of annual earnings. For each fiscal year the analyzer uses diluted earnings per share according to Yahoo Finance, i.e. earnings divided by that year's share count. If that figure is missing for a fiscal year, it falls back on Yahoo Finance's other data. For companies filing annual reports with the SEC, it has checked this figure since 2 November 2026 against diluted earnings per share from the most recent annual report filed there that covers the year, per traded share or per ADR. If the two differ by more than 2%, the SEC figure applies, provided it matches the earnings and share count filed there. This picks up later restatements that Yahoo Finance has not adopted, for example after the introduction of the accounting standard IFRS 17 (Lloyds and HSBC 2022). The analyzer only uses the SEC figures if they agree with Yahoo Finance to within 1% in at least two and at least half of the years covered by both. Their longer history is not used yet. Until 2 November 2026 it divided earlier years' annual earnings by today's share count: for companies buying back shares, earnings per share of earlier years came out too high and the P/E median too low; for companies issuing new shares, the other way round. The median only covers the months from the first of these annual figures on; in between, the analyzer connects the annual figures in a straight line. It does not calculate earnings backwards for the time before that. Since the shortest period is five years, the selected period therefore usually only changes the visible price history, not the median. Only monthly values above 3 enter the median, and no upward outliers: the limit is 2.5 times the median, at least 50. In the analyzer the median is shown as "Ø" or "avg.", in the sidebar and in the tooltip of the fair value line together with the month it starts from. If Yahoo Finance provides fewer than three positive annual earnings figures or the median is above 60, the P/E row in the quality check shows no average either, and the P/E light then follows the sector P/E. For royalty companies the sector P/E does not fit, so the light then shows a gray "N/A". Until 1 November 2026 the analyzer usually calculated earnings before the first annual figure backwards using past earnings growth (at most 7% per year) and, in the default period, took the median over ten years, using only monthly values above 7. Older analyses state fair values based on that earlier calculation.
  • P/CF, P/B and P/S: P/CF works like P/E: the median covers the months from the first annual figure of Yahoo Finance's cash flow series on (also about four years) and only appears with at least three years of positive operating cash flow. It only includes monthly values above 2 and below an upper limit between 20 and 30. If operating cash flow over the last twelve months is positive but more than four times that of the last fiscal year with positive cash flow, or less than a quarter of it, for example at some banks, the analyzer only uses the twelve-month figure and shows no average. For P/B and P/S, "Ø" or "avg." stands for the median of the monthly values over the selected period, in the sidebar over ten years. Book value and revenue per share are only available at the balance sheet dates of roughly the last one and a half years; for the time before that, the analyzer uses the oldest of these values.
  • Earnings quality: If a large part of earnings does not come from operations, for example from investments, interest or asset sales, P/E, return on equity, PEG and fair value look too favourable. The analyzer measures what share of pre-tax income over the last twelve months is not part of operating income according to Yahoo Finance. Above 25%, it marks the P/E and a high return on equity as "distorted", shows no PEG, the fair value indicator states the share instead of "fairly valued" or "below fair value", and the assessment says so unless a sector text takes precedence. Whatever already looks expensive on full earnings stays marked as expensive. For orientation it usually shows an operating P/E and an operating return on equity, both rough approximations. The rule does not apply to banks, insurers and other financial companies, to conglomerates or to Strategy (MSTR), where investment income cannot be cleanly separated from the business. Berkshire Hathaway is the exception: its earnings have included unrealized gains and losses on the stock portfolio since 2018, so the analyzer always treats them as distorted.
  • PEG: P/E divided by the company's own earnings growth over recent years (annual rate, capped at 40%). The analyzer does not use analyst estimates for it.
  • Alien assessment: rule-based text modules depending on sector, special case and metrics. They describe where the metrics stand against their history and what matters in the sector. They are not a recommendation to buy or sell and not a review of the individual case.
  • The data can be wrong or incomplete. The analyzer shows a notice for known data problems, but it does not catch every error.
