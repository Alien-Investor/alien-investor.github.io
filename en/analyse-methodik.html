How my analyses are made

by Alien Investor · as of 30 Oct 2026

This page explains how my stock analyses, my Bitcoin analyses and the Alien Analyzer work: where the numbers come from, what is fact and what is my assessment, which assumptions sit behind a fair value, and where I have interests of my own. Every analysis links to this page.

What an analysis is and what it is not

  • An analysis is my personal assessment of a company or of Bitcoin, based on publicly available data.
  • It is not investment advice. I do not know your financial situation, your goals or your risk tolerance, and I do not take them into account. No analysis says that a stock or Bitcoin suits you.
  • Analyses are not updated continuously. The date stated in the analysis applies. Prices, metrics and my assessment may change afterwards.
  • Stocks and Bitcoin can fluctuate sharply. Losses up to a total loss are possible.

Facts, estimates, opinion

Every analysis contains three kinds of statements. I keep them apart:

KindExamplesHow it is marked
FactsRevenue, earnings, debt, dividends, segment figuresfrom annual reports and mandatory filings, with the data date
EstimatesFair value, price scenarios, forecasts, analyst expectationslabelled as a model calculation or estimate, with the assumptions; third-party forecasts with source
OpinionMoat, management, risks, the Alien verdictworded as my assessment

Values I cannot back up with a source are marked as an estimate or as not available, instead of inventing a number.

Sources

  • Primary sources: annual reports, annual and quarterly filings and other mandatory disclosures (for US companies via SEC EDGAR), the companies' investor relations pages, official statistics.
  • Metrics and price history: my own Alien Analyzer V2, which processes data from Yahoo Finance. This data can contain errors, for example from one-off accounting effects or financial data in a different currency than the share price. I check conspicuous values against the annual reports and say so in the text when doubts remain.
  • Research: research tools, including AI-assisted ones, help with collecting and sorting. The primary sources are what counts: numbers and factual statements are checked against them before publication.
  • Blog posts and forums are not a source for numbers.

How I value

Fair value

The fair value in my analyses is a model calculation, not a price target. It is based on the fair value indicator of the Alien Analyzer:

Fair value = current earnings per share × median P/E ratio over the last ten years, in the months with actual earnings figures (usually all ten years with annual reports at the SEC, otherwise about four)

The assumptions behind it:

  • The market will value the company in the future roughly as it did on average in the past.
  • Current earnings are representative of the company's earning power.

Both can be wrong: for cyclicals at an earnings peak or trough, after one-off effects, when growth breaks or when the interest rate environment changes. That is why I check the indicator in every analysis against the P/E history of recent years and put it into context in the text instead of taking it over unchecked. When an analysis names a price scenario, its assumptions are stated in the text.

Metrics and quality check

For context I use historical valuation multiples (P/E, P/CF, P/S, P/B), the dividend history and a quality check covering return on equity, debt, free cash flow, equity ratio, earnings growth, dividend yield and the change in share count. The traffic-light colours in the analyzer follow fixed rules: mostly fixed thresholds, for P/E a comparison with the stock's own average and the sector P/E, and special rules for some sectors. The P/E light shows "GOOD" when the P/E is more than 15% below both, and "HIGH" when it is more than 20% above the stock's own average or the sector P/E. Exception: if it is more than 15% below its own average and at the same time more than 20% above the sector P/E, the light has shown "FAIR" since 5 November 2026, before that "HIGH" (Rollins on 4 November 2026: P/E 35.2, own average 53.9, sector P/E 20). If it is more than 20% above its own average and more than 15% below the sector P/E, it stays "HIGH". In all other cases it shows "FAIR". Without a reliable own average (see P/E median) and for mining and metals companies only the sector P/E counts, for royalty companies only the stock's own average. For banks, insurers, REITs, commodity companies and other special cases, some metrics do not fit. The analysis says so. For banks and BDCs, the quality check does not rate free cash flow ("N/A"): at banks, operating cash flow swings mainly with trading assets, and BDCs book the purchase of their investments there. The investment banks Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley also count as banks.

Bitcoin analyses

Bitcoin is not a stock. There are no earnings and no P/E ratio. My Bitcoin analyses are based on the price history, on the power law model and on publicly available market data.

  • Trend: price in USD = 1.0117 × 10-17 × (days since 3 January 2009)5.82 (power law model after Giovanni Santostasi, in a widely used calibration; newer estimates give a slightly lower exponent).
  • Support: 0.42 × trend (floor from the same calibration). Between support and trend lies the corridor in which the price has often moved so far.
  • The model describes the past. No mechanism guarantees that it will keep holding. Price scenarios from the model are estimates.

I hold Bitcoin. Every Bitcoin analysis says so as well.

Conflicts of interest

  • Every analysis has a dated disclosure block: whether I or persons closely associated with me hold shares of the company, and whether I have a business relationship with it.
  • I receive no money from the companies I analyse. The only exception is Amazon: I receive royalties for my books through Kindle Direct Publishing. The Amazon analysis says so.
  • My income: books (Amazon and direct sales), affiliate links (marked with *), donations and services. The YouTube channel is not monetised.
  • Requested analyses: A reader pays for an analysis of a stock of his choice. He only chooses the company. He has no influence on content or result, and his personal situation plays no role. A paid analysis is marked as such in the disclosure.

Date and timeliness

The top of every analysis states the data date. Prices and metrics refer to that point in time. The disclosure block states the date and time of publication, after a full revision also the time of that revision, plus the date of the disclosure itself.

Alien Analyzer

The Alien Analyzer V2 calculates automatically for every ticker you enter. It knows neither you nor your goals.

  • Data: Yahoo Finance, fetched through my own server. For companies that file annual reports with the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), i.e. US stocks, ADRs and some home listings of such companies such as SAP.DE, ASML.AS or 6758.T, the server also uses the XBRL data from these reports (see P/E median as well as P/CF, P/B and P/S). Your browser does not query any third-party data services. The quality check states the date of the balance sheet behind the equity ratio and P/B. It can be older than the earnings data. If a company reports in a different currency than its share price, such as Chinese ADRs in renminbi or Novo Nordisk in Danish kroner, the analyzer converts earnings per share, book value, revenue and cash flow into the price currency using exchange rates from Yahoo Finance, the history at the rate of each month. The free cash flow row names the original currency. If Yahoo Finance delivers part of the financial figures in a different currency than the rest, the analyzer first converts these values into the currency of the other figures at the exchange rate of the respective reporting date. For Petrobras this affects balance sheet and cash flow, for Vale also earnings: they come in US dollars, the rest in reais.
  • Fair value indicator: formula as above. Current earnings per share are the earnings of the last twelve months according to Yahoo Finance. After the last annual report, the analyzer does not extrapolate with a growth rate but connects it to this twelve-month figure. If the price is more than 15% above or below fair value, the analyzer shows "above" or "below fair value", otherwise "fairly valued". For comparison, the P/E fan shows earnings per share times 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30. For REITs and mining and metals companies the analyzer shows no fair value, because the P/E ratio is of little use there, nor when the median P/E is above 60, when there are fewer than three consecutive annual earnings figures (see P/E median) or when the company made a loss over the last twelve months.
  • P/E median: For each month the analyzer divides the price by earnings per share. For each fiscal year it uses diluted earnings per share according to Yahoo Finance, i.e. earnings divided by that year's share count. Yahoo Finance provides about four years. If that figure is missing for a fiscal year, it uses the figure from the annual report filed with the SEC, otherwise it falls back on Yahoo Finance's other data. For companies filing annual reports with the SEC, it has checked this figure since 2 November 2026 against diluted earnings per share from the most recent annual report filed there that covers the year, per traded share or per ADR. If the two differ by more than 2%, the SEC figure applies, provided it matches the earnings and share count filed there. This picks up later restatements that Yahoo Finance has not adopted, for example after the introduction of the accounting standard IFRS 17 (Lloyds and HSBC 2022). The analyzer only uses the SEC figures if they agree with Yahoo Finance to within 1% in at least two and at least half of the years covered by both. If only one year is covered by both, that year is enough if it agrees and its share count confirms the ratio between the two figures (Toyota ADR). Since 4 November 2026 the series reaches further back for these companies: before the Yahoo Finance years, the analyzer adds the earlier fiscal years from the SEC filings, going back year by year until one is missing or its figures do not fit together. Fitting together means that reported earnings per share are within 2% of earnings divided by the share count (or of the company's usual ratio between the two), or differ from it only by their rounding (Ford reported USD 0.01 for 2019, calculated 0.0117). If a company reports earnings per share in cents, the analyzer converts them (Rio Tinto 2015 to 2017). For a loss year it is enough that earnings and earnings per share are negative; there is no P/E there anyway. Without annual reports filed with the SEC the series stays at about four years (for example Nestlé, LVMH, Siemens), and the same applies if the SEC figures are missing or fail the check (TSMC). Between two annual earnings figures the analyzer connects the values in a straight line, including across a single loss or missing year. If two or more such years lie in between, there is no monthly value there, and only the years after that gap count towards the minimum of three annual earnings figures (see below). It does not calculate earnings backwards for the time before the first annual earnings figure. For fair value, sidebar and quality check the median always covers the last ten years, and the selected period only changes the visible price history there; in the Multiples tab, as for P/CF, P/B and P/S, the selected period applies. Only monthly values above 3 enter the median, and no upward outliers: the limit is 2.5 times the median, at least 50. In the analyzer the median is shown as "Ø" or "avg.", in the sidebar and in the tooltip of the fair value line together with the month it starts from. If there are fewer than three consecutive annual earnings figures or the median is above 60, the P/E row in the quality check shows no average either, and the P/E light then follows the sector P/E. For royalty companies the sector P/E does not fit, so the light then shows a gray "N/A". Until 4 November 2026 the median only covered the Yahoo Finance years, even for companies filing with the SEC: for SAP 38.2 instead of 29.7, for AT&T 11.2 instead of 9.1 (as of 4 November 2026). Until 2 November 2026 it divided earlier years' annual earnings by today's share count: for companies buying back shares, earnings per share of earlier years came out too high and the P/E median too low; for companies issuing new shares, the other way round. Until 1 November 2026 the analyzer usually calculated earnings before the first annual figure backwards using past earnings growth (at most 7% per year) and, in the default period, took the median over ten years, using only monthly values above 7. Older analyses state fair values based on that earlier calculation.
  • P/CF, P/B and P/S: "Ø" or "avg." stands for the median of the monthly values over the selected period, for P/B and P/S in the sidebar over ten years. The analyzer calculates book value, revenue and operating cash flow per share for each fiscal year using that year's share count and connects the annual figures in a straight line. After that come the latest quarterly balance sheets as well as revenue and operating cash flow over the last twelve months, each divided by today's share count. It does not calculate anything backwards for the time before the first annual figure. It takes equity, revenue and operating cash flow for the last four or so fiscal years from Yahoo Finance, earlier ones from the annual reports filed with the SEC, if they agree with Yahoo Finance to within 1% in at least two and at least half of the years covered by both. For revenue this is often not the case, for example at oil and gas companies, banks, brokers and utilities, because revenue is defined differently in the SEC data. Without SEC figures the average therefore usually only starts in 2023. If it starts later than ten years ago for P/B or P/S, the sidebar states the month from which it applies. The analyzer only shows an average from three fiscal years on (for P/CF with positive operating cash flow), for P/B only if equity is positive today, and none if today's share count differs from that of the last fiscal year by more than a factor of two (for example at Interactive Brokers, whose listed shares represent just over a quarter of the business). If in that case the latest balance sheet confirms the share count of the last fiscal year, it divides equity for the current P/B by the share count of that balance sheet instead of today's. There is no P/CF between a fiscal year with negative operating cash flow and the adjacent years. For all three ratios the average includes all positive monthly values. If the median is above 100, the analyzer shows none. For P/CF it also shows none for banks, since their operating cash flow moves with balance sheet items such as trading assets, loans and deposits. The current P/S is the price divided by revenue per share over the last twelve months, the current P/CF the price divided by operating cash flow per share over the last twelve months (or of the last fiscal year if Yahoo Finance has no more recent twelve-month figure, both at today's share count). Until 2 November 2026, book value and revenue per share were only available at the balance sheet dates of roughly the last one and a half years; for the time before that, the analyzer used the oldest of these values. The average thus mainly followed the share price and was usually too low. A current P/S above 2.5 times the average was replaced by the average. Until then the P/CF average only covered Yahoo Finance's four or so fiscal years, divided by today's share count, and only included monthly values above 2 and below an upper limit between 20 and 30. For highly valued stocks it was therefore too low (Hermès) or missing entirely (Nvidia, Tesla, Eli Lilly). Until 3 November 2026 the analyzer capped every monthly P/B and P/S value as well as the current P/B at 50. If more than half of the months were above that, the average showed 50.0 (Home Depot's P/B over ten years instead of 63.6).
  • Dividend yield: The analyzer divides the dividends of the last twelve months by the current price; for the yield line it divides the dividends of the twelve months up to the end of each month by that month's price. The ex-date counts. So that a payment shifted by a few days does not drop out of the window, the window reaches back a quarter of the usual gap between two payments further (median of the last three years). If a payment in the window is followed by another one about a year later, only the newer one counts: a quarterly payer thus gets four payments, not five. Special dividends count. Amounts are gross, before withholding tax and depositary fees. If there was no payment in the last twelve months, the analyzer shows the yield Yahoo Finance reports itself, otherwise "–". In the quality check the light shows "strong" from 3%, "fair" from 1% and "low" below that. Dividend growth compares today's twelve-month sum with that of one, two, five and ten years ago, over several years as an annual rate. A special dividend in either period feeds through in full (Costco, ex-date 27 December 2023: 15 US dollars); without a payment in either period it shows "–". For the US-traded securities of 14 foreign companies (Petrobras, Vale, SAP, ASML, Novo Nordisk, Sony, Toyota, Alibaba, TSMC, Tencent, Equinor, Franco-Nevada, OR Royalties, Wheaton Precious Metals) it converts the dividends of the home exchange: amount per share times shares per US security times the exchange rate on the ex-date. The dividend tab then names the home exchange. The reason is gaps in Yahoo Finance's US series: for Petrobras individual instalments and the December 2025 distribution are missing, for the Sony and Toyota ADRs every payment since April 2025, for Franco-Nevada, OR Royalties and Wheaton two quarters of 2025. For the Tencent ADR, the in-kind distributions of JD.com and Meituan shares (2022 and 2023) also appear as cash dividends. Since 5 November 2026 the same applies to 27 Canadian companies whose shares trade in New York and Toronto, among them Enbridge, Canada's five largest banks, Canadian Natural, Fortis and Pan American Silver: their US series are missing one or two payments from August 2025 to January 2026 (Enbridge 4.3 instead of 5.7%, as of 5 November 2026). For Waste Connections, Magna, Gildan and the Brookfield partnerships BEP and BIP the US series stays, because the Toronto series has errors of its own there. If the price of the US security is not between two thirds and one and a half times the converted home price, for example after a change in the ADR ratio, the US series stays. Until 3 November 2026 the analyzer added up the most recent payments; how many (one, two, four or twelve) it derived from the average gap of the last eight payments. For irregular payers the yield was therefore off (as of 2 November 2026: Vale 15.6 instead of 9.0%, Volkswagen preferred 16.5 instead of 7.5%, Tencent ADR 2.5 instead of 1.3%), and Stellantis still showed 16.2% after its last payment in April 2025. On top of that it requested dividends in monthly steps, in which Yahoo Finance was missing the payments of whole years. Both together: Main Street Capital 1.8 instead of 7.7%, Itaú ADR 1.2 instead of 7.9%. The Petrobras ADR came to 4.9 instead of 6.7%.
  • Earnings quality: If a large part of earnings does not come from operations, for example from investments, interest or asset sales, P/E, return on equity, PEG and fair value look too favourable. The analyzer measures what share of pre-tax income over the last twelve months is not part of operating income according to Yahoo Finance. Above 25%, it marks the P/E and a high return on equity as "distorted", shows no PEG, the fair value indicator states the share instead of "fairly valued" or "below fair value", and the assessment says so unless a sector text takes precedence. Whatever already looks expensive on full earnings stays marked as expensive. For orientation it usually shows an operating P/E and an operating return on equity, both rough approximations. The rule does not apply to banks, insurers and other financial companies, to conglomerates or to Strategy (MSTR), where investment income cannot be cleanly separated from the business. Berkshire Hathaway is the exception: its earnings have included unrealized gains and losses on the stock portfolio since 2018, so the analyzer always treats them as distorted.
  • PEG: P/E divided by the company's own earnings growth over recent years (annual rate, capped at 40%). The analyzer does not use analyst estimates for it.
  • Alien assessment: rule-based text modules depending on sector, special case and metrics. They describe where the metrics stand against their history and what matters in the sector. They are not a recommendation to buy or sell and not a review of the individual case.
  • The data can be wrong or incomplete. The analyzer shows a notice for known data problems, but it does not catch every error.
